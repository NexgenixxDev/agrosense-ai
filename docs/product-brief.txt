 AgroSense AI project proposal and Codex
 build brief
 An AI powered crop health advisor for smallholder farmers in Namibia

 Prepared for Martin Kristof • 29 September 2026

 Status: proposed product and implementation brief, subject to field validation


 1 Executive proposal
 We propose AgroSense AI, an Android-first mobile application supported by a web portal for agricultural advisors and
 administrators. A farmer photographs an affected crop, answers a few simple questions, and receives possible
 causes, practical next steps, and an option to request expert review. The app also keeps crop records, supports
 follow-up photographs, and provides water-management reminders appropriate to the farmer’s circumstances.

 The proposed problem is that smallholder farmers lose crops to diseases, pests, and water stress, while timely
 agricultural advice may be difficult to obtain. The extent of these losses and the delay in accessing extension support
 in the intended pilot communities must be established through research. This proposal does not claim that local
 interviews, market sizing, or field trials have already been completed.

 The first release should demonstrate a complete working journey from photo capture to a real AI response, approved
 advice, expert review, and follow-up. It should be usable on affordable smartphones and tolerate interrupted
 connectivity. Its role is to support early decisions and access to advice; a photograph alone cannot confirm every
 disease or determine an exact irrigation requirement.

 Proposed positioning: locally relevant crop support that combines visual AI, reviewed guidance, and human
 follow-up for farmers in Namibia.

 3 Evidence and problem validation
 What published evidence supports
 The Namibian Agronomic Board identifies mahangu, white maize, and wheat as staple grain crops within its
 agronomy work [1]. This supports treating mahangu as an important local product requirement, even if reliable AI
 coverage for it is unavailable initially.

 FAO describes weather variability and the importance of climate-resilient practices in Namibia. It also notes
 constraints on irrigation for smallholder dryland production [2]. The product should therefore distinguish rain-fed
 farming from irrigated farming instead of assuming every user can water a crop on demand.

 FAO’s integrated pest management guidance supports considering several control approaches and limiting
 unnecessary pesticide use [3]. AgroSense should prioritize reviewed, context-appropriate guidance rather than
 automatically recommending a chemical for every photo.

 These sources establish context. They do not establish the percentage of Namibian harvest losses caused by
 delayed advice, the number of addressable smartphone users, or willingness to pay.

 Proposed primary research
 Interview approximately 15-20 farmers, 3-5 agricultural advisors, and 2-3 cooperative or agricultural programme
 representatives before fixing the pilot scope. These are proposed research sample sizes, not completed interviews or
 statistically representative national estimates.

 Ask farmers which crops they grow; whether production is rain-fed or irrigated; how they recognize crop problems;
 whom they ask for help; how long advice takes; what happened in their most recent affected season; what phone,
 language, and data access they have; and whether they would use a photo-based service. Ask permission before
 recording personal details or crop images.

 Ask advisors which problems can be assessed visually, which require field or laboratory confirmation, which crop
 treatments are appropriate locally, and how many digital cases they could realistically review. Ask potential
 institutional buyers what outcomes and reporting would justify a paid pilot.

 Record interview date, participant category, anonymized notes, supporting evidence, and limitations. Separate a
 farmer’s estimated loss from a measured loss. Select one pilot cluster with a committed advisor and accessible
 farmers; northern crop-growing areas are candidates, not confirmed launch sites.

 5 Crop coverage and language
 Create a crop catalogue that can represent mahangu, maize, sorghum, and common horticultural crops. This
 catalogue is not a promise that the AI recognizes every crop or condition.

 For the technical MVP, start with tomato and maize only if a suitable licensed model or dataset supports the selected
 conditions. Display the exact supported conditions in the app. Include mahangu in field records, reviewed learning
 material, and advisor referrals from the beginning; enable automatic mahangu diagnosis only after a suitable model
 has passed local evaluation. Confirm the final pilot crop selection with farmers and advisors.

 Start with plain English and translation-ready screens. Select the first additional local language through pilot research,
 for example Oshikwanyama or Oshindonga for a relevant community. Do not label every language variant as a
 completed translation or present machine-translated agronomic instructions as reviewed. Human-reviewed audio
 guidance is a later accessibility enhancement.


 6 Farmer application
 Use four main navigation items: Home, Check Crop, My Farm, and Help. Keep the most important action, “Check
 my crop,” visible on the home screen.

 First use
 Explain the service in a few short screens. Allow browsing of general guidance without registration. Require an
 account before submitting a case to the server or contacting an advisor. Use phone verification when a real SMS
 provider is configured; development authentication must be visibly separate and disabled in production.

 Ask for preferred language, region, and the crops grown. Precise location is optional. Allow manual region selection
 when location permission is denied. Farmers can add a field name, crop, planting date if known, and rain-fed or
 irrigated status.

 Never interpret a healthy-looking photograph as proof that the entire crop is disease-free. Do not describe an AI
 suggestion as an expert-confirmed diagnosis.

 Low-connectivity behaviour
 Offline users can capture drafts, view saved cases, access downloaded guidance, and receive local reminders. New
 cloud analysis waits for connectivity. Show “Saved on this phone - waiting to upload” rather than a simulated result.

 Compress images while preserving visible symptoms, with an initial target of 300-700 KB per photo and a retake or
 higher-quality option where detail is insufficient. These are design targets to test, not guaranteed diagnostic limits.
 Use retry with backoff and idempotent submissions. Let the user delete an unsent draft or retry a failed upload.

 Provisional test device: Android 10 or later with 2 GB RAM. Reassess this minimum after checking actual pilot phones
 and dependencies. Keep animations light, allow text enlargement, and use buttons approximately 48 logical pixels
 high or larger. Do not rely on colour alone for status.

 Visual direction
 Use a warm, agricultural theme: dark green #166534, light background #F7FAF5, dark text #17251C, and amber
 #B45309 for caution. Verify contrast in implementation. Use Arial throughout the app and web portal, approximately
 16-18 logical pixels for body text, clear crop illustrations, familiar icons with text labels, and short sentences. Show
 one main task per screen.


 7 Advisor and administrator web system
 The advisor dashboard contains an assigned-case queue, filters by crop and case state, and a case detail view. Case
 details include farmer-provided symptoms, permissioned photos, AI candidates, reviewed advice shown, and previous
 follow-ups. Advisors can request another photo, record a correction with a reason, and issue a response.

 Maintain separate labels for AI suggestion, advisor reviewed, and laboratory confirmed. The last label requires
 recorded supporting evidence. If no advisor is assigned or available, communicate that clearly and show only verified
 contact alternatives. Do not promise immediate responses without staffing.

 The administrator dashboard manages accounts, advisor assignments, crop support, model versions, advice
 publication, translations, and service health. Content moves through draft, review, publication, and retirement. Retain
 version history so each farmer result can be traced to the advice and model used at that time.

 An initial report should show submissions, processing failures, supported versus unsupported crops, review requests,
 response times, follow-up completion, and cost per analysis. A map or counts of submissions must not be described
 as measured disease prevalence.

 8 AI design and safeguards
 The specific AI task
 Use supervised computer vision to classify supported crop-condition combinations from photographs. The pipeline
 combines image-quality checks, crop context, an image classifier or specialized inference provider, and a
 deterministic decision layer. Symptom answers support triage and expert review; do not claim the model uses them
 unless the selected model actually accepts and evaluates those inputs.

 AI is appropriate for visual pattern recognition at scale. Scheduling, permissions, notifications, guidance publication,
 and most water reminders should use ordinary software rules. A general chatbot is not required for the MVP.

 Inference pipeline
  1. Validate the uploaded file type, dimensions, size, and decoded image content.

  2. Check for unusable images or non-plant subjects and request a retake where appropriate.

  3. Verify that the selected crop and requested assessment are supported.

  4. Run real inference through a replaceable provider adapter.

  5. Apply calibrated acceptance and abstention rules. Handle unknown conditions and conflicting evidence.

  6. Retrieve a published advice card matching the crop and suggested condition.

  7. Return the candidate causes, uncertainty state, advice version, model version, and escalation option.

 Model confidence is not automatically a probability of diagnostic correctness. Do not invent confidence percentages or
 use an LLM’s self-reported certainty as calibrated confidence. Hide numeric scores from farmers until validation
 supports a clear interpretation. Never describe a fixed threshold such as 80% as scientifically validated merely
 because it is configured.

 For initial development, use either a licensed crop-diagnosis API whose coverage has been checked or a trained
 image classifier served by the AI service. Decide after checking licensing, cost, supported crops, data handling, and
 field performance. Plantix advertises a crop-diagnosis API, but access, pricing, and Namibia-specific suitability require
 verification [6].

 Data and evaluation
 Use properly licensed public data for a baseline, then gather consented local field photos with qualified labels. Record
 dataset provenance, licensing, crop, condition, region, collection context, and label confidence. Public benchmark
 performance must not be presented as Namibian field accuracy. Published research illustrates that performance can
 deteriorate substantially when test images differ from training conditions [4].

 Split training, validation, and test data by farm and capture session where possible; remove duplicates and
 near-duplicates across splits. Include varied lighting, backgrounds, devices, healthy samples, unsupported conditions,
 water stress, and non-plant images. Keep an independent local test set and publish per-class sample counts,
 precision, recall, confusion matrix, and abstention rate. Do not use farmer feedback as automatically correct training
 labels.

 Proposed supervised-pilot gate: a qualified reviewer agrees that each enabled class has adequate local evidence;
 accepted predictions achieve at least 90% precision on the held-out local set, with sample counts and uncertainty
 reported. This is a proposed target, not measured performance. Low coverage cannot be hidden by rejecting most
 images: report acceptance rate and recall alongside precision. Classes without enough evidence remain expert-only.

 Treatment guidance
 Store advice in a reviewed knowledge base, not as unrestricted generated text. Prefer observation, prevention, and
 appropriate integrated pest management options. A generated explanation, if added later, must stay within approved
 content and retain references.

 Do not generate pesticide names, doses, mixing instructions, or harvest intervals from model memory. Chemical
 recommendations require a qualified reviewer to verify the current local product authorization and crop-specific label;
 record that source and date. If verified guidance is unavailable, withhold chemical instructions and refer the case. This
 is a product safety requirement, not a claim that regulatory verification has already occurred.

 Water management
 A photo cannot determine soil moisture or an exact water requirement. The MVP supports manual watering logs,
 observation prompts, and user-scheduled reminders. Any advice rule must account for rain-fed versus irrigated
 production and available inputs. Weather integration is a later enhancement unless a provider is selected and tested;
 show forecast source, timestamp, location, and missing-data states. Do not invent litres per plant or automatic
 watering schedules from photos.

 11 Business case and financial validation
 Proposed customer and revenue model
 Farmers are the primary users. Cooperatives, NGOs, agricultural programmes, or other institutions could sponsor
 access and advisor capacity. Test institution-funded access first, with core farmer use free during the pilot. Possible
 paid services are programme dashboards, managed onboarding, and contracted support. These are commercial
 hypotheses; no partner agreement or willingness to pay is established.

 Plantix already offers photo-based crop diagnosis [5]. AgroSense cannot credibly claim the idea is new. Proposed
 differentiation is Namibia-specific crop coverage, locally reviewed guidance, language support,
 constrained-connectivity workflows, and accountable advisor follow-up. Test these differences against actual
 alternatives before claiming superiority. Existing calls, messaging groups, field visits, and extension services are also
 alternatives.

 Market sizing method
 Do not use the entire population of Namibia as the market. Obtain a dated official count of relevant crop-farming
 households and document definitions and source year. Estimate smartphone access and service eligibility through
 appropriate evidence, then define the reachable pilot population with partners.

  • TAM: eligible crop-farming households within the intended national scope.

  • SAM: households meeting crop, region, device, language, and access requirements supported by the product.

  • SOM: farmers realistically reachable through signed or evidenced distribution arrangements over the first year.

 Report household and individual farmer measures separately. Do not multiply unrelated national percentages and
 describe the result as an observed farmer count. Current numerical market size remains a research gap.

 For a first pilot, propose approximately 30-50 consenting farmers and 2-3 advisors for 6-8 weeks, subject to capacity

 13 MVP acceptance criteria
  1. A farmer can register in the configured environment, add a field, capture a photo, submit it, and reopen the
     persisted result after restarting the app.

  2. At least one explicitly supported crop-condition set uses real inference. Test fixtures are clearly marked and never
     used as a hidden production fallback.

  3. Blurry, non-plant, unsupported, and uncertain inputs have appropriate retake, abstention, or review flows.

  4. An offline draft survives app restart and uploads once after reconnecting; repeat requests do not create duplicate
     cases or billable jobs.

  5. Every displayed treatment card is published, versioned, sourced, and traceable to a reviewer. No model-generated
     chemical instructions appear.

  6. A farmer can request review; an assigned advisor can respond; the farmer can retrieve the response even if push
     delivery fails.

  7. A farmer cannot access another farmer’s data by changing a URL or identifier. Advisors see only their authorized
     scope.

  8. A denied camera, storage, location, or notification permission results in a usable alternative or clear explanation.

  9. Processing retries, provider timeouts, expired uploads, and notification failures have recoverable states and useful
     logs.

 14 Instructions to give Codex
 Copy the following instruction with this file attached to your coding session.

     Build AgroSense AI using this proposal as the product and technical specification. The required deliverables are
     a Flutter Android farmer app, a React TypeScript advisor and administrator portal, a NestJS API with
     PostgreSQL, and a replaceable Python AI service. Start by inspecting the existing repository and its instructions,
     if any, and preserve unrelated work.

     Implement the MVP in working vertical slices. Begin with a farmer adding a field, capturing a crop photo, saving a
     case, running real inference for an explicitly supported crop, and receiving reviewed guidance. Then add the
     advisor review journey, offline draft synchronization, reminders, and content administration. Do not stop at static
     screens or a frontend backed only by fake results.

     Before implementation, write a short architecture plan and record assumptions. Use the proposed stack unless
     the existing project justifies a change; explain material changes. Pin compatible dependencies, use migrations
     and a documented OpenAPI contract, and supply environment examples without secrets. Keep the mobile, web,
     API, worker, and AI components independently runnable.

     Implement an inference adapter with explicit real and development-fixture modes. Never invent model weights,
     dataset access, diagnostic accuracy, confidence percentages, weather readings, advisor availability, or API
     responses. If an external credential or licensed model is missing, complete all unblocked implementation and
     show an honest unavailable state. List the exact missing dependency. Do not call the AI-integrated MVP
     complete until the real inference path has been exercised and documented.

     Use approved, versioned knowledge-base content for advice. Clearly label sample content as development-only.
     Production publication requires a reviewer. Include uncertain and unsupported outcomes, human review, offline
     capture, idempotent uploads and processing, permission checks, and clear error states from the beginning. Do
     not automatically enable mahangu diagnosis without suitable evidence.

     Create meaningful tests for authorization, case-state transitions, duplicate synchronization, AI contract validation,
     abstention, and advice publication. Run end-to-end checks for the farmer-to-advisor journey. Provide a clearly
     labelled sample dataset for interface testing, developer setup instructions, Android build instructions, a test
     summary, and an honest list of outstanding limitations. Do not implement deferred features unless separately
     requested.

 Demonstration script
 Show a farmer opening the app, adding a tomato field, and taking a photo. Run real inference and show its saved
 result with reviewed advice. Submit an unsupported crop and demonstrate the referral path. Disable connectivity,
 save another crop check, restart the app, reconnect, and show exactly one uploaded case. Request advisor review,
 respond through the web portal, and show the farmer receiving the response. Finish with a follow-up observation and
 the administrator’s traceable advice history.
